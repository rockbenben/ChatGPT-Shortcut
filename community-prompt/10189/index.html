Scout had lost her mother since she could remember, and it was mentioned in the book that “there was no trace of mother in her earliest memories” (Chinese ed. p.6, English ed. p.9). This growth environment lacking maternal love made her break away from the demonstration and discipline of traditional female roles too early, and she was more inclined to form behavioral traits under the influence of male relatives [5 The Modalities and Construction of Female Consciousness in To Kill a Mockingbird]. At home, the children were mainly educated by their father Atticus, while Calpurnia, a Black maid, took charge of their daily life. This family structure was completely different from the traditional “men govern outside, women govern inside” model in the Southern society at that time, thus allowing Scout to be free from many constraints brought by gender division of labor. In the process of raising children, Atticus took on both the responsibilities of a father and a mother. He did not conduct differentiated training on his children according to the gender stereotypes of the Southern society, and always adhered to gender-neutral behavioral requirements and ability cultivation for Scout. He let her not have to follow the behavioral norms exclusive to women, and the brother and sister had completely equal growth opportunities at home (Chinese ed. p.34, English ed. p.11). In Maycomb County, traditional Southern families often required girls to be gentle, quiet and polite, but life in the Finch family focused on practicality, and Atticus valued cultivating his children’s character and cognition more. He insisted on reading to Scout and Jem every day, enabling the children to develop the ability of independent thinking. He even admitted that “reading is as natural and necessary as breathing” (Chinese ed. p.34, English ed. p.10), and never deliberately asked Scout to learn feminine etiquette, posture, words and deeds. This confirms Atticus’ respect for his children’s nature as a father [7 An Interpretation of Scout’s Female Growth in To Kill a Mockingbird]. In addition, there were no direct companionship of female elders such as grandmothers or mothers in the Finch family, which also reduced the daily teaching of traditional Southern female moral standards and behavioral norms. It was not until Aunt Alexandra moved in to live with them that Scout felt gender discipline from a female elder for the first time. The aunt straightforwardly required her to “put aside those boyish behaviors and be a real lady” (Chinese ed. p.83, English ed. p.83). This was the first time in Scout’s growth that she was clearly constrained by female norms, which also indirectly indicated the lack of gender constraints in her early growth. The relaxed and equal atmosphere in the Finch family also gave Scout a growth space for free expression and independent action. The brother and sister directly called their father “Atticus” instead of following the traditional generational address in the South. This detail directly reflects the equal relationship without hierarchy or gender differences in the family (Chinese ed. p.6, English ed. p.9). Atticus never scolded them for this address, but always got along with the children with an equal attitude. Atticus’ education never took gender as a standard. He placed Scout and Jem in the same educational environment, giving them the same right to express, think and act, making Scout form a simple cognition from an early age that “gender cannot be used as a standard to judge ability and rights” (Chinese ed. p.34, English ed. p.10). Some scholars believe that the core of Atticus’ education is to guide his children to view the world with a just and inclusive attitude. This educational philosophy not only shaped Scout’s moral outlook, but also made her get rid of the double prejudices of gender and race [11 On the Children’s Narrative Perspective in To Kill a Mockingbird]. In daily communication and interaction, Atticus always communicated with Scout with an equal attitude. When Scout had a conflict with Miss Maudie because of the rigidity of school education and poured out her grievances to her father, Atticus did not ask her to be obedient and silent just because she was a girl. Instead, he encouraged her to speak out her thoughts boldly, and used a core sentence to guide her to learn to put herself in others’ shoes: “You never really understand a person until you consider things from his point of view—until you climb into his skin and walk around in it” (Chinese ed. p.83, English ed. p.84). This direct teaching not only made Scout learn to be considerate of others, but also made her understand the importance of independent thinking and insisting on expressing herself, laying a solid ideological foundation for her to break through gender discipline. Atticus never deliberately shaped Scout into a “weak” girl, but instead encouraged her to explore the world independently. And he himself always treated neighbors and people of different races equally. Even in Maycomb County, where racial discrimination was deeply rooted, he still resolutely defended Tom Robinson, a Black man, and said, “In our courts, when it’s a white man’s word against a Black man’s, the white man always wins. They’re ugly, but those are the facts of life... But before I can live with other folks I’ve got to live with myself. The one thing that doesn’t abide by majority rule is a person’s conscience” (Chinese ed. p.277, English ed. p.85). Atticus’ words and deeds became the most vivid educational example, allowing Scout to get rid of the traditional Southern concepts of gender hierarchy and racial superiority and inferiority through osmosis, and gain the spiritual strength to break through gender constraints. Jem was four years older than Scout, but he never restricted her with gender standards just because she was his younger sister, nor did he treat her as a weak person who needed to be taken care of. Instead, he often took her to climb trees, explore the Radley house, play role-playing and other outdoor activities that boys usually played. The book repeatedly describes that the brother and sister “explored around the town all summer and turned Maycomb upside down” (Chinese ed. p.6, English ed. p.9). These summer adventures became their daily life, and also made Scout naturally develop a cheerful and courageous character in her actions, far away from the Southern traditional requirements of women being gentle and quiet. This gender-neutral way of getting along made Scout gradually integrate male courage and perseverance into her character, forming a sharp contrast with the rigid image of a Southern lady [5 The Modalities and Construction of Female Consciousness in To Kill a Mockingbird]. They inevitably had conflicts and running-in in their daily interaction, but they always quarreled and reconciled in an equal way. For example, when they had differences over the act of exploring the Radley house, Jem never asked Scout not to participate on the grounds that “you are a girl and should be quiet and not take risks”; Scout also said frankly, “Jem never treats me like a little girl” (Chinese ed. p.34, English ed. p.11). This allowed Scout to not suppress her nature to cater to her gender identity, and under the imperceptible influence of Jem’s boyish thinking and behavior, she formed a gender-neutral cognition, believing that gender is not the key to determining behavior. Scout once said that she “would rather climb trees and play in the mud with Jem than sit on the porch embroidering and gossiping like the other girls in town” (Chinese ed. p.83, English ed. p.84). This inner monologue is a direct reflection of her breaking away from female stereotypes. To sum up, Scout’s rebellious character against traditional female norms is closely related to her family environment. The absence of maternal love and the special family structure of the Finch family made her get rid of the early gender discipline of traditional Southern women; Atticus’ gender-neutral education concept and equal communication attitude not only respected her nature, but also shaped her correct cognition of gender equality and laid a ideological foundation for her to break through gender constraints; the gender-neutral way of getting along with her brother Jem made her develop a cheerful and courageous character, and further strengthened her resistance to female stereotypes. All these factors together promoted the formation of Scout’s rebellious character, made her keep a distance from the rigid image of a Southern lady, and laid an important foundation for the germination and subsequent awakening of her female consciousness. Chapter 1 1.2 Scout’s Unconscious Resistance to Traditional Southern Female Norms Scout’s unconscious resistance to traditional Southern female norms is clearly reflected in her external clothing choices, daily behavior patterns and language expression styles. This not only constitutes the core feature of her “tomboy” image, but also is the most intuitive manifestation of her gender consciousness in the germination stage. The formation of this image is closely related to Atticus’ free and tolerant educational method. It is precisely this kind of education that allows Scout to break free from the traditional Southern gender constraints and shape her personality according to her own nature [2] (An Interpretation of Scout’s Female Growth in To Kill a Mockingbird). In terms of clothing choices, Scout has an instinctive resistance to traditional female clothing such as dresses. Aunt Alexandra once required her to wear dresses and behave elegantly, believing that “if I wore breeches, I would never become a lady, never in a million years”, while Scout said, “I can’t do anything in a dress”. In her opinion, a dress is not an external symbol of female identity, but a shackle that restricts the body and behavior—such clothes directly limit outdoor free activities such as running, jumping and climbing, which are incompatible with the lifestyle she yearns for (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.68 / English ed. p.72). For this reason, Scout took the initiative to choose more practical boyish clothing such as trousers and shirts, putting comfort and freedom of movement first in dressing, completely ignoring the rigid norm of the Southern society that requires girls to “wear dresses”. Even in the face of persuasion and correction from female elders such as Calpurnia and Aunt Alexandra, she always insisted on her own clothing choices, and even said frankly, “A person can be sunny even in trousers”, clearly expressing her resistance to gender clothing discipline through direct and firm external behaviors (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.69 / English ed. p.73). Scout’s “masculine” tendency in clothing choice is not a deliberate gender dislocation in childhood, but a natural choice based on her own feelings and living needs. However, this choice objectively breaks the inherent expectations of the Maycomb County for women’s external image, which is also the natural dissolution of the traditional Southern lady norms in children [4] (An Analysis of the Growth Factors of Scout in To Kill a Mockingbird). In terms of behavior patterns, Scout completely abandoned the criterion of gentleness and quietness that the Southern society required women to abide by, and took fields, woods and streets as her main activity places. She explored and played games with her brother Jem and Dill, and took the Radley house as the “ultimate object of adventure career”, inventing the Radley Legend game. They even competed to “see who could run alone to the house at dusk, touch the peeling paint on the outer wall, and then run back screaming”. Scout fully released her childlike nature in running, jumping, chasing and exploring, completely ignoring the town’s expectations for girls’ behavior (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.18 / English ed. p.21). Scout refused to learn women’s housework and gentle demeanor, and had no interest in the “boudoir life” that Southern women were disciplined to live. On the contrary, she often went “out for a walk with Jem with air guns to see if they could meet a hare or a squirrel”, and found her true self in outdoor activities full of wildness and freedom (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.45 / English ed. p.48). Her lively, active and brave behaviors form a strong contrast with the quiet image of traditional Southern women. This persistence in outdoor adventures is essentially Scout’s pure way as a child to resist being incorporated into the established female behavior framework, and an important manifestation of her unconscious resistance to traditional female behavior norms. This also made her a “tomboy” in the eyes of the people of Maycomb County who completely deviated from female behavioral norms, and this unrestrained character also laid an important internal foundation for the subsequent awakening of her female consciousness [5] (The Growth Course of Scout in To Kill a Mockingbird). In terms of language expression, Scout’s straightforwardness stands in sharp contrast to the Southern society’s requirement for women to be implicit and gentle. Traditional gender discipline requires women to speak modestly and implicitly, and some even teach them that “it’s not necessary to say everything you know. It’s very unladylike—and besides, people don’t like people around them who know more than they do”. But Scout always maintained the candor and directness of a child, and never deliberately concealed her true thoughts just because she was a girl (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.136 / English ed. p.140). Faced with Uncle Jack’s misunderstanding and punishment, she retorted straightforwardly: “You’re not fair, Uncle Jack. You’re real nice, but you’re too busy looking at the outside of things. You didn’t even give me a chance to tell you my side of it—you just started whippin’ me”, and stated her reasons clearly: “You never stopped to give me a chance to explain myself—you just hit me” (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.105 / English ed. p.109). In the face of others’ prejudices and injustice, she would also express her dissatisfaction directly, saying frankly, “Nobody has the right to talk to people like that—it makes me sick” (To Kill a Mockingbird, Chinese ed. p.135 / English ed. p.139). Whether communicating with teachers and neighbors, or getting along with her family, Scout directly expressed her views and feelings. Even if her words seemed childish or even sharp, she was unwilling to cater to the world’s expectations for women’s expression. This unadorned straightforwardness is not a deliberate rebellion or confrontation, but a natural state in which she has not been assimilated by gender discipline. However, in the traditional context of Maycomb County, this constitutes a silent resistance to the requirement of implicit expression for women, becomes an important feature of her “tomboy” image, and highlights her true self not bound by secular discipline. This true self-expression is the external manifestation of the germination of her independent personality [3] (A Study on the Guide Image in To Kill a Mockingbird from the Perspective of Bildungsroman). Scout deviated from the traditional Southern female norms in all aspects of clothing, behavior and language expression. These behaviors were her natural choices based on her own feelings and nature in childhood. She did not have a clear sense of resistance, but objectively formed an unconscious resistance to the established gender discipline, and also laid the groundwork for the subsequent germination of her gender consciousness and the awakening of her female consciousness. The shaping of this starting point of growth is precisely the result of the collision between Atticus’ equal educational concept and the traditional Southern gender education [1] (On the Children’s Narrative Perspective in To Kill a Mockingbird). She said, “No, Jem, I think there’s just one kind of folks. Folks.” (Chinese ed. p.277, English ed. p.232). This simple cognition allowed her to break the racial and class shackles of the town. In daily communication, Scout naturally accepted Calpurnia’s lifestyle and behavioral norms, and never had the slightest estrangement from Calpurnia because of her skin color.
